## Core Frameworks

### The Three Feedback Triggers (Thanks for the Feedback — Stone & Heen)
When criticism lands hard, it's usually activating one of three triggers:
- **Truth trigger**: "That's not accurate" — you dispute the content
- **Relationship trigger**: "You're the wrong person to say this to me" — you're reacting to who said it, not what they said
- **Identity trigger**: "This means I'm fundamentally flawed" — the feedback feels like a verdict on your worth, not a comment on a behavior

Identifying which trigger you're in changes what you need to do with the reaction.

### The Useful vs. Useless Distinction
Not all criticism is worth the same response. Before spending emotional energy:
- **Is there a kernel of truth?** Even harsh or badly delivered feedback can contain something real
- **Is this a pattern across multiple sources?** One person's criticism is data; the same criticism from three different people is a signal
- **Is the person qualified to assess this?** Weight feedback accordingly — not all critics have the relevant knowledge or standing

### The Separation Technique
When criticism stings, there are two things happening simultaneously:
1. The **emotional response** — hurt, anger, defensiveness — which is valid and needs to be processed
2. The **informational content** — is there something actionable here? — which needs to be assessed

You can't do both at the same time. Process the emotion first, then evaluate the content.

## Key Books

- **Thanks for the Feedback** — Douglas Stone & Sheila Heen: The definitive book on receiving feedback; why it's harder than giving it
- **Mindset** — Carol Dweck: Fixed mindset treats criticism as a verdict; growth mindset treats it as data for improvement

---

## Tactical Principles

1. **You don't have to respond right away**: "I'll need to think about this" is a complete answer — you're not required to react in real time
2. **The emotional sting is not proof that the criticism is wrong**: The most accurate feedback often hurts the most, because it's close to something real
3. **Look for the 10% that's true**: Even unfair or poorly delivered criticism usually has a grain. Find it, extract it, discard the rest
4. **Distinguish the observation from the character judgment**: "You missed the deadline" is feedback; "You're unreliable" is a character attack — you engage with the first differently from the second
5. **Don't argue in your head**: The internal replay loop isn't processing — it's avoidance. Write it out, talk it through with someone, or name what trigger is activated and let that be enough

## Sample Scripts

**Receiving criticism in the moment:**
> "I appreciate you telling me that. I want to think about it properly before I respond — can I come back to you on this?"

**When the internal loop won't stop:**
> "I've been replaying this for days. Let me actually write out: what's true in this? What's unfair? What am I going to do about the true part? Once I've answered those three questions, I don't need to keep circling it."

**Responding to feedback you're going to act on:**
> "I've thought about what you said, and I think you're right that I've been too quiet on progress updates. I'm going to [specific change]. Thank you for telling me — I know it wasn't easy to say."

**Responding to criticism that was unfair or badly delivered:**
> "I've heard your feedback. I don't fully agree with the way it was framed, and I want to come back to that when we both have more time. But I'm taking seriously the part about [X]."

**Self-compassion after receiving hard criticism:**
> "This hit me harder than I expected. That doesn't mean it's true, and it doesn't mean it's false — it means I care. I can sit with this without needing to decide right now whether to defend myself or collapse."
