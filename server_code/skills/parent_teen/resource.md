## Core Frameworks

### What's Happening in the Teenage Brain
Adolescence involves profound neurological change — the prefrontal cortex (responsible for impulse control, long-term thinking, and consequence evaluation) isn't fully developed until age 25. Meanwhile, the limbic system (emotion and reward) is in overdrive.

This means:
- Risk-taking and emotional intensity are neurologically normal, not character flaws
- Teens are genuinely less able to reason about long-term consequences in the heat of the moment
- The peer group becomes the primary reference point — this is biologically driven, not rejection of you

Understanding this doesn't mean accepting dangerous behavior — it means calibrating your expectations and your approach.

### Connection Before Correction
Research on adolescent development (Siegel, Steinberg) consistently shows: **the parent-teen relationship is the most powerful protective factor** against risky behavior, mental health problems, and poor outcomes.

Rules without connection breed rebellion or shutdown. Connection creates the trust that makes rules meaningful.

**Practical implication**: When things are bad between you, investing in connection (not more rules) is usually the more effective move.

### The Autonomy Paradox
Teens pull away in order to develop a self. This pulling away is healthy — it's not rejection of you, it's the biological work of individuation. The parent's job shifts from protection to *staying available as a safe base* while allowing more independence.

The harder you hold on, the harder they push. Creating space — while maintaining your presence — keeps the door open.

## Tactical Principles

1. **Listen without immediately fixing or lecturing**: When a teen shares something, your first response shapes whether they share again — curiosity before advice
2. **Pick your battles**: Not every rule is worth the relationship cost — distinguish between safety issues and preference issues
3. **Stay regulated yourself**: Teens are emotionally contagious; your own calm is a co-regulation tool
4. **Find the side-by-side moments**: Car rides, cooking together, watching something — teens often open up when you're not facing each other, not in a formal "sit down, let's talk" setting
5. **Repair after conflict**: "I came in too hot yesterday and I'm sorry" models something important and rebuilds trust

## Sample Scripts

**Opening a conversation when they've pulled back:**
> "I'm not going to lecture you. I just miss you. Can we do something together this weekend? Doesn't have to be a big thing."

**After they said something hurtful during a fight:**
> "I know things got heated and some things were said. I want to get back to okay with you. Can we start over?"

**When you're worried about something specific:**
> "I need to say something because I'd rather be wrong and awkward than say nothing and have something happen. I've noticed [X], and I'm worried about you. I'm not accusing you of anything — I just want to know you're okay."

**When they say "you don't understand":**
> "You're right that I don't know exactly what it feels like to be you right now. But I'm trying to. Tell me more — I'm not here to judge."

**When rules aren't working:**
> "I don't want us to keep fighting about [issue]. Help me understand what's going on for you, and let's figure out what reasonable actually looks like."
