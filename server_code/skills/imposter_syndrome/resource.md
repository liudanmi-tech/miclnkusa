## Core Frameworks

### What Imposter Syndrome Actually Is
First named by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes (1978), imposter syndrome is the persistent internal experience of intellectual fraudulence — feeling like you don't deserve your success and will eventually be exposed.

Key facts:
- It is more common among high achievers, not low performers
- It is more common in environments where people feel like "firsts" (first in family to go to college, underrepresented in their field, new to a level)
- **It is not correlated with actual competence** — it's a feeling, not a fact

### The Competence–Confidence Gap
High performers often set their internal benchmark at "mastery" — the feeling of fully knowing something. But competence and confidence don't develop at the same rate. You can be objectively good at something and still feel uncertain — especially in new contexts.

The problem: imposter syndrome interprets the *feeling* of uncertainty as evidence of incompetence. It isn't.

### The Attribution Asymmetry
People with imposter syndrome have a specific pattern:
- **Success** → attributed to luck, timing, other people, catching a break
- **Failure** → attributed to fixed personal deficiency ("I'm not smart enough")

Rebalancing this attribution is the core work:
- When something goes well: ask "What did *I* specifically do that contributed to this?"
- When something goes badly: ask "What was situational? What was learnable?"

- **Mindset** — Carol Dweck: Fixed vs. growth mindset; reframing uncertainty as learning rather than exposure

## Tactical Principles

1. **Name it**: "This is imposter syndrome talking" creates distance from the thought — you're observing it, not being it
2. **Collect evidence deliberately**: Keep a running list of things you've done well — your brain won't do this automatically, you have to do it on purpose
3. **Normalize not knowing**: "I don't know yet" is different from "I'm a fraud" — curiosity is what competence looks like from the inside, not certainty
4. **Ask the question**: The fear of asking is almost always worse than the asking — most people respect curiosity and self-awareness
5. **Compare to your past self, not to others' exteriors**: Everyone looks more confident from the outside — you're comparing your interior to their exterior

## Sample Scripts

**Reframing a spiral in the moment:**
> "I'm having imposter thoughts right now. That's a feeling, not a fact. I was hired because I earned this. Not knowing everything on day [X] doesn't mean I'm in the wrong place — it means I'm in a place where I'm still growing."

**Asking a question in a meeting (fighting the avoidance):**
> "I want to make sure I'm understanding this correctly — can you walk me through [X] one more time?" *(Most people in that room probably needed the same clarification.)*

**Talking to a mentor or trusted colleague:**
> "I've been feeling like everyone around me gets things more intuitively than I do — like I'm playing catch-up. Is that something you've experienced? I'm trying to figure out how much of it is real and how much is noise."

**After a success you're tempted to dismiss:**
> "Before I move on, I want to acknowledge that I actually did something hard there. It worked because of [specific things I did] — not just luck."
