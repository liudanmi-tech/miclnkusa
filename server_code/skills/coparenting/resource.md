## Core Frameworks

### The Core Principle: Business Partnership, Not Friendship
Effective co-parenting doesn't require liking your ex — it requires treating the parenting relationship like a professional partnership:
- Communicate about the child's needs, schedule, and wellbeing — not about your feelings toward each other
- Keep adult conflict out of child-facing spaces
- Decisions are evaluated by one standard: what's best for the child, not what's most convenient or who "wins"

You don't have to like them. You do have to work with them.

### The BIFF Method (Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm)
For written co-parent communication (text, email):
- **Brief**: Keep it short — long messages invite arguments
- **Informative**: Stick to facts and logistics — "pickup is at 3pm Saturday"
- **Friendly**: Neutral to warm tone — not cold, not sarcastic
- **Firm**: Clear about what you're proposing or need

If you receive a hostile message, respond only to the logistics, not the emotional charge.

### Protecting Children from Adult Conflict
Children absorb parental conflict even when it's not directed at them. Research (Emery, 2012) consistently shows that conflict between co-parents is more damaging to children than the separation itself.

**Rules of thumb:**
- Don't speak negatively about the other parent in front of the child — ever
- Don't use the child as a messenger
- Don't ask the child to choose sides or report on the other household
- If the child brings up the other parent in a loaded way, respond neutrally: "Sounds like you had a good time with Dad"

## Key Books

- **The Co-Parenting Handbook** — Karen Bonnell: Practical frameworks for communication, transitions, and protecting kids from conflict
- **Two Homes, One Childhood** — Robert Emery: Research-based guide to raising children across two households; how to reduce conflict's impact

## Tactical Principles

1. **Keep adult grievances in adult spaces**: Therapist, trusted friend, journal — not in front of or through the child
2. **Respond to co-parent behavior you can control**: You cannot change how they parent in their home — focus on your household and your consistency
3. **Consistency where possible helps children**: Shared bedtime routines, homework expectations, and discipline approaches — even imperfect alignment is better than chaos
4. **New partners are a real issue — and need a real conversation**: Introducing a new partner to young children too quickly is a legitimate concern; raising it once, calmly and focused on the child's adjustment, is appropriate
5. **Document when needed**: If conflict escalates or custody disagreements arise, written records of communication matter

## Sample Scripts

**BIFF message about a schedule change:**
> "Hi — can we switch this Saturday's pickup to 4pm instead of 3? I have a commitment until 3:30. Happy to do a swap if that works better for you. Let me know."

**When they say something negative about you to the child (raising it with them):**
> "I want to bring something up that's about [child]'s wellbeing, not our relationship. She mentioned you said [X] about me. I'm not raising this to start a fight — I'm raising it because it puts her in a hard position. Can we agree to keep adult stuff between us?"

**When parenting styles conflict:**
> "I've noticed we handle [behavior] differently, and I think it might be confusing for her. I'm not saying my way is the only way — I'm wondering if we could land on something consistent enough that she knows what to expect."

**When you're upset after a difficult exchange:**
> Before responding to a hostile message, ask: "What does my child need from this response?" Answer that question — not the emotional provocation.
