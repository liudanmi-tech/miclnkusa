## Core Frameworks

### The Dual Frame Problem
Immigrant parents and their children are often operating from entirely different frames of reference:
- **Parents' frame**: The country they left, the sacrifices made to get here, the survival-mode logic of immigration, what success meant in their context
- **Child's frame**: The country they grew up in, different norms around individualism, career, relationships, and self-expression

Neither frame is wrong — they're just describing different realities. Most conflict happens when each side assumes the other is being unreasonable, when actually they're being perfectly rational within their own frame.

**The bridge**: Translate between frames rather than argue about which is right.

### The Sacrifice Narrative
Many immigrant families carry a "sacrifice narrative" — the explicit or implicit message that the child's success is the return on the parents' suffering. This creates a specific kind of guilt and pressure:
- You feel like choosing your own path is a betrayal
- Ordinary life choices become loaded with moral weight
- Parents may not realize how heavy this framing is for their children

Naming this internally — "this is the sacrifice narrative activating, not an actual moral failing" — creates space to respond from a calmer place.

### Translating, Not Convincing
The goal in these conversations is usually not to convince your parents that your way is right. It's to help them understand your world well enough to worry less.

Instead of: "UX design is a real career"
Try: "Here's what I'd actually earn, what companies hire for this, what the job security looks like" — give them data in the language they trust (stability, income, respect)

## Key Books

- **The Immigrant Family Therapy** — Monica McGoldrick: How family systems carry cultural narratives across generations; what gets lost and gained in translation
- **Minor Feelings** — Cathy Park Hong: The specific emotional experience of second-generation Asian Americans navigating identity, expectation, and belonging

## Tactical Principles

1. **You can hold both truths**: Their sacrifices were real *and* you get to choose your own life — these aren't in conflict
2. **Validate the fear under the pressure**: Most parental pressure is anxiety in disguise — "I need you to be safe" looks like "you must be a doctor"
3. **Use their language where possible**: If stability matters to them, lead with stability data. If respect/face matters, name the social status of what you do
4. **You don't have to convince them — you may just need to inform them**: Some parents come around when they see you succeeding, not when you argue
5. **The guilt is not proof of wrongdoing**: Guilt is a response trained into you — it's not a reliable signal that you're making the wrong choice

## Sample Scripts

**Reframing a career conversation:**
> "I know you're worried that this isn't a stable path — I want to show you what it actually looks like. Companies like Google, Apple, and thousands of others hire UX designers at [salary range]. I've done the research and I'm not going in blind. Can I walk you through it?"

**Acknowledging the sacrifice without surrendering to it:**
> "I know how much you gave up to bring us here, and I don't take that lightly. I want to honor that by building a life I'm fully committed to — and I can only do that if it's a life I actually chose. That's not rejection; that's me taking your sacrifice seriously."

**When they say "you've become too American":**
> "I grew up here — this is just who I am. Being [cultural identity] and being shaped by where I grew up aren't opposites. I hold both."

**When the conversation escalates:**
> "I don't want us to fight about this. I love you, and I want you to understand my life. Can we slow down and try again?"
