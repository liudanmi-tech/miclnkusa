## Core Frameworks

### Family Coming Out Is Different from Friend Coming Out
Coming out to family carries unique weight:
- **History**: Family relationships have decades of accumulated meaning — your coming out lands in a context that includes everything they've hoped for, assumed, and feared about your life
- **Dependency**: You may still rely on them financially, emotionally, or practically — which changes the risk calculus
- **Identity on both sides**: For many parents, especially in religious or traditional families, your coming out also disrupts their own identity as parents and their vision of your future

This doesn't make it wrong to come out — it means going in with clear eyes about the complexity.

### Safety Is the First Question, Always
Before anything else:
- If coming out could result in being cut off financially while you're still dependent on them — have a plan B
- If there is any risk of physical harm — do not come out in that situation; your safety comes before the relationship's honesty
- Resources exist: The Trevor Project, PFLAG, campus LGBTQ+ centers — know what's available

### The Reaction Is Not the Final Answer
Especially in religious or traditional families, the first reaction is often shock, fear, or grief — not their considered position. Many parents who react badly initially come around over months or years, especially when they see that their child is still the same person they've always loved.

This doesn't mean you have to accept a bad reaction or wait indefinitely — it means a hard first conversation isn't always the end of the story.

### PFLAG Framework: What Parents Often Need
Many parents go through their own process:
1. **Shock / disbelief**: "This can't be right"
2. **Guilt / blame**: "Where did we go wrong?"
3. **Fear**: "What will their life be like?"
4. **Bargaining**: "Maybe it's a phase"
5. **Grief**: Mourning the future they'd imagined
6. **Acceptance**: Coming to see you fully

They are not going to arrive at step 6 the day you tell them. Understanding that they're on their own journey — separate from your journey — helps you not take their early response as a final verdict.

## Tactical Principles

1. **You don't owe anyone a particular explanation**: What you share, how much, and when is entirely yours to decide
2. **Tell one person first**: Having even one person in the family who knows and is supportive before the bigger conversations makes everything easier
3. **A letter is a legitimate option**: Especially with parents — it gives them time to react privately before responding to you, which often produces a more considered response
4. **You can pause a conversation**: "I don't need your response right now — I just needed you to know" takes the pressure off both of you in the moment
5. **Non-acceptance from a parent is a real loss**: You're allowed to grieve that, even while knowing you did nothing wrong

## Sample Scripts

**Telling a parent:**
> "Mom, I want to tell you something about myself that I've been holding for a while. I'm [identity]. I'm telling you because I love you and I don't want to keep hiding this. I'm not asking you to have a perfect response right now — I just need you to know."

**If they ask "are you sure?"**
> "Yes. This isn't something I arrived at quickly — I've known for a long time."

**If the reaction is tears or silence:**
> "I know this is a lot. You don't have to say anything right now. I'm still the same person. I love you."

**If the reaction is angry or rejecting:**
> "I hear that you're upset. I'm not going to argue tonight — I'm going to give you some time. I hope we can talk more when things feel calmer."

**If they bring up religion:**
> "I know your faith is important to you, and mine is [your relationship to it]. I'm not asking you to abandon your beliefs — I'm asking you to stay in relationship with me while you figure out how to hold both."

**Checking in weeks later if they've gone quiet:**
> "I know things felt hard after our conversation. I've been giving you space, but I want you to know I'm still here, and I'd really like to find our way back to each other."
