## Core Frameworks

### Coming Out Is Yours
There is no right time, right way, or right amount of explanation required. Some things worth knowing:
- You don't owe anyone a "reason" for who you are
- You can share as much or as little as you want to in any given conversation
- Coming out is a process, not a single moment — you'll do it many times with many people throughout your life
- You are the expert on your own safety and readiness — no one else can tell you when it's the right time

### Safety First
Before any coming out conversation where the reaction is uncertain:
- **Financial dependence**: If you're dependent on someone who might withdraw support, plan for that possibility — campus resources, LGBTQ+ organizations, and housing support exist
- **Physical safety**: If there's any risk of an unsafe reaction, prioritize your safety above anything else — coming out can wait, your safety cannot
- **Emotional safety**: Having a supportive person on standby (a friend, a counselor) for after the conversation helps

### Handling Difficult Reactions
People's first reactions are often not their final position. Initial shock, silence, or even negative words from someone who loves you doesn't always mean permanent rejection — though it still hurts and you don't have to minimize that.

What to do with a hard initial reaction:
- You don't have to have the entire conversation in one sitting
- It's okay to say "I don't need you to have a response right now — I just needed you to know"
- Give them time, but also give yourself permission to lean on others in the meantime

What to do if someone responds badly and digs in:
- You are not responsible for managing their discomfort
- You are allowed to end the conversation and revisit it later
- You are allowed to choose how much access to you someone gets

---

## Tactical Principles

1. **You're not asking for permission**: You're sharing something about yourself — their approval is not required for the reality of who you are
2. **Prepare for the range, not just the best or worst case**: Think through: what if they're immediately wonderful? What if they need time? What if it goes badly?
3. **A support person on standby helps**: Someone who knows you're having the conversation that day and can be there afterward — text, call, or in person
4. **You can do it in writing if in-person feels too hard**: A letter or message gives them time to process privately before responding — that's not a lesser option
5. **Your timeline is the right timeline**: Coming out on someone else's schedule, or because you feel you "should" — that's pressure you don't have to accept

## Sample Scripts

**Telling a parent in person:**
> "I've been wanting to tell you something for a while, and I've been nervous about it. I'm gay. I'm not looking for you to have a perfect response right now — I just needed you to know this about me."

**If they ask "how long have you known?"**
> "For a while. I took time to understand it myself before sharing it. I wanted to be sure before I told you."

**If the reaction is shocked or quiet:**
> "You don't have to say anything right now. I know this might need some time. I'm still me — I just wanted you to know this part of me too."

**If the reaction is negative:**
> "I hear that this is hard for you. I'm not going to argue about who I am. I love you, and I hope you'll come around. But I also needed to stop hiding this."

**Telling a close friend:**
> "There's something I've wanted to share with you. I'm [identity] — I've known for a while and you're one of the first people I've told. I wanted you to know because you matter to me."
