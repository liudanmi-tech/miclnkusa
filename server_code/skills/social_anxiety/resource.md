## Core Frameworks

### The Social Anxiety Cycle
Social anxiety perpetuates itself through a predictable loop:
1. **Anticipatory anxiety**: Dreading the situation before it happens, imagining worst cases
2. **Safety behaviors in the situation**: Avoiding eye contact, over-preparing, staying quiet, saying little — these reduce anxiety briefly but prevent disengagement
3. **Post-event processing**: Replaying what happened, fixating on perceived failures, building a case for "I did badly"
4. **Avoidance**: Avoiding similar situations next time to prevent the anxiety — which keeps the anxiety alive and growing

**The key insight**: Avoidance maintains anxiety. Gradual, intentional exposure — done at your pace — is what weakens it.

### The Spotlight Effect (Gilovich, 1999)
Research consistently shows that people dramatically overestimate how much others notice their anxiety, mistakes, and awkward moments. You are not under a spotlight. Other people are mostly occupied with their own experience.

Your blush, your voice shake, your stumbled sentence — others notice it far less than you fear.

### The Two Cognitive Distortions
Social anxiety runs on two errors:
- **Mind-reading**: "They think I'm boring / weird / incompetent" — you don't actually know what they think
- **Fortune-telling**: "This is going to go terribly" — the actual outcome is almost always better than the prediction

When you notice these, name them: "That's mind-reading." "That's fortune-telling." Then ask: "What's the evidence? What actually happened last time?"

## Key Books

- **Overcoming Social Anxiety and Shyness** — Gillian Butler: CBT-based workbook; practical tools for challenging the cognitive and behavioral patterns
- **The Anxiety and Phobia Workbook** — Edmund Bourne: Comprehensive; covers relaxation, cognitive restructuring, and gradual exposure

## Tactical Principles

1. **Approach, don't avoid**: Every avoided situation strengthens anxiety; every approached situation — even imperfectly — weakens it
2. **Behavioral experiments over reassurance-seeking**: Instead of asking "will it be okay?" — go and find out. The data from experience is more powerful than any reassurance
3. **Reduce safety behaviors gradually**: Safety behaviors (staying quiet, over-preparing, leaving early) provide relief but prevent learning — reducing them, even slightly, accelerates progress
4. **Defuse from post-event rumination**: When you catch yourself replaying, ask "What would a fair observer say about how that went?" — not your inner critic
5. **Professional support works**: CBT for social anxiety has strong evidence — if this is significantly limiting your life, therapy is worth pursuing

## Sample Scripts

**Before a dreaded event:**
> "I'm dreading this, and that's okay. Dread doesn't mean it'll go badly — it means my anxiety is activated. I've been in situations like this before and gotten through them. I'm going to go, and whatever happens, I'll learn something."

**In the moment when anxiety spikes:**
> Take one slow breath. Shift attention outward — focus on the other person, not on yourself. Anxiety shrinks when you stop monitoring it.

**Reframing a post-event rumination spiral:**
> "I'm doing the replay. My brain is going to keep finding evidence that it was bad — that's what anxious brains do. But what would a neutral person say if they watched that? Probably: 'You showed up. You participated. That's it.'"

**Gradual exposure step (speaking up in meetings):**
> Start with one sentence per meeting — just one. "I want to add something small to what [X] said." Build from there.

**Talking to someone about it:**
> "I deal with social anxiety — it's something I'm working on. I wanted you to know because sometimes I might seem quiet or reserved in groups, and I didn't want you to read that as disinterest."
