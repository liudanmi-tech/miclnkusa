# Resource: Supporting a Friend in Crisis

> Injected at: skill activation, alongside baseline_text in system prompt
> Token budget: ≤400 tokens

---

## Core Frameworks

### Presence Over Solutions
The most common mistake when supporting someone in crisis is trying to fix it. The urge to problem-solve comes from love — but it often signals to the person that their pain is something to be resolved rather than witnessed.

What people in crisis usually need most:
1. To feel less alone
2. To be heard without judgment
3. To know you're not going anywhere

The most powerful thing you can often say is not advice — it's "I'm here. What you're going through is really hard."

### The Empathy vs. Sympathy Distinction (Brené Brown)
- **Sympathy**: Looking down from above — "At least it's not worse" / "You'll get through this" — creates distance
- **Empathy**: Climbing down into the feeling with them — "That sounds incredibly hard. I can see why you're exhausted." — creates connection

Empathy doesn't require you to have the same experience. It requires you to acknowledge theirs.

### When to Gently Suggest Professional Help
Knowing when to encourage therapy or professional support is one of the most useful skills:
- When they're not functioning (not eating, not leaving the house, not sleeping)
- When the same pain keeps repeating without movement
- When they're dealing with something beyond what friendship can hold (trauma, suicidal thoughts, clinical depression)
- When you've become their only support and you're feeling the weight

How to suggest it: plant the seed gently, more than once if needed, without pressure or ultimatums.

### If There's a Safety Concern
If a friend expresses thoughts of suicide or self-harm:
- Take it seriously — ask directly: "Are you having thoughts of hurting yourself?"
- Asking does not plant the idea — it opens a door
- Stay with them (virtually or in person) and help connect them with a crisis line or emergency support
- In the US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988)

## Tactical Principles

1. **Ask what they need, don't assume**: "Do you want to talk about it, or would it help more to just hang out and not talk about it?" gives them agency
2. **Show up in small, consistent ways**: A daily text matters more than one big gesture — presence over time signals "I'm not leaving"
3. **Avoid the minimizing phrases**: "At least..." / "Everything happens for a reason" / "You should be grateful for..." — these invalidate, even when well-meaning
4. **Take care of your own capacity**: You cannot sustainably support someone if you're running empty — you're allowed to have limits
5. **You are not their therapist**: Your job is friendship, not treatment — knowing that distinction protects both of you

## Sample Scripts

**Checking in without pressure:**
> "Hey — no need to explain anything. I'm just thinking about you and wanted you to know I'm here. If you want to talk, I'm available. If you just want company without talking, that works too."

**When they say "I'm fine" but aren't:**
> "I hear you. I also care about you too much to just take that at face value. You don't have to tell me anything — I just want you to know the door is open."

**Suggesting professional support:**
> "I've been thinking about you a lot. What you're carrying sounds really heavy — more than one person should have to hold alone. Have you thought about talking to someone, like a therapist? I'm not going anywhere either way — I just want you to have more support than just me."

**When you're feeling helpless:**
> "I don't know what the right thing to say is, and I'm sorry I can't fix this. But I'm not going anywhere. You're not alone in this."

**Setting a limit on your own capacity (kindly):**
> "I love you and I want to be here for you. I also need to be honest that I'm feeling a bit stretched right now. Can we figure out how to stay connected in a way that works for both of us?"
