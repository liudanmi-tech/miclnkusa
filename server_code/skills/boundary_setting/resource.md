## Core Frameworks

### What a Boundary Actually Is
A boundary is not a wall, a punishment, or a rejection. It's a statement about what you will and won't do — based on your values, needs, and capacity.

The confusion: people often treat boundaries as things you impose on others ("you can't do X"). In reality, a boundary is about your own behavior: "If X happens, I will do Y."

- "You can't call me after 9pm" → not a boundary (you can't control what they do)
- "If you call after 9pm, I won't answer" → a boundary (you're describing your action)

The power of this shift: you stop waiting for the other person to comply and start controlling your own response.

### The Guilt-Boundary Connection
Guilt after setting a limit is nearly universal for people who've been conditioned to prioritize others. But guilt is not a reliable moral guide here — it's a conditioned response.

Ask three questions when guilt shows up:
1. "Did I say anything untrue?" (If no: the guilt isn't about honesty)
2. "Did I say anything unkind?" (If no: the guilt isn't about harm)
3. "Am I hurting them, or just not giving them what they want?" (These are different)

If you can answer no to the first two and yes to the third, the guilt is old wiring — not a signal that you did something wrong.

### The Boundary-Setting Sequence
1. **Know your limit** (before any conversation): What specifically is crossing a line for you?
2. **State it simply**: One sentence — "I'm not available for calls after 9pm"
3. **Don't over-explain**: One reason is enough; more reasons invite negotiation
4. **Hold it consistently**: Inconsistent limits teach people they can push through

## Key Books

- **Set Boundaries, Find Peace** — Nedra Tawwab: Practical, compassionate; breaks down every category of boundary with real-world language
- **The Disease to Please** — Harriet Braiker: The psychology of approval addiction; why people-pleasers struggle to have limits and how to change

## Tactical Principles

1. **You don't need permission to have limits**: You don't need to justify, negotiate, or get the other person to agree that your boundary is fair
2. **"No" is a complete sentence**: You can add context if you want to, but you don't owe an explanation
3. **A limit without a consequence isn't a limit**: If the behavior continues and there's no response, you've communicated that the limit isn't real — follow through matters
4. **People who respect you will respect your limits**: The people who react badly to you having needs are showing you something important about the dynamic
5. **Start with low-stakes practice**: Say no to small things to build the muscle before the high-stakes moments

## Sample Scripts

**Ending a call you didn't want:**
> "Hey — I have to go, I have [thing] I need to get to. I'm glad we talked. Let's catch up properly soon." *(You don't need to explain that "thing" further.)*

**With a friend who calls daily in crisis:**
> "I care about you and I want to be here for you. I also have to be honest — I can't do daily calls right now, it's affecting my own wellbeing. Can we plan one good check-in a week where I'm actually fully present for you?"

**Declining a request at work:**
> "I can't take this on right now — I'm at capacity. I want to be useful to you so I'm telling you before I take it on and do it poorly."

**When someone says "you've changed" after you set a limit:**
> "I have. I'm getting better at knowing what I can actually give. That's a good thing for both of us."

**Internal check-in before a hard conversation:**
> "What do I need to say? One sentence. What is the simplest, truest version of what I need here?" *(Then say that, and stop.)*
